START HERE · NO ACOUSTICS BACKGROUND NEEDED

Why can a room fill the silence between words?

You say a short word. One copy travels straight to the listener. Other copies bounce off surfaces and arrive later. These copies overlap with the next word. We will describe that process using samples, impulse response, convolution, signal energy, Fourier analysis and STFT.

1 · Predict

If surfaces absorb more sound, should the first arrival change—or should the later copies become weaker?

2 · Observe

Change one setting. Watch the response and cumulative energy curve. Compare the same seat before and after.

3 · Explain

Use the measured change to support a claim. A shorter energy tail is evidence about this signal, not proof of better exam scores or word recognition.

Course concepts are the tools. The classroom is our application. Acoustic conventions such as C₅₀ and D₅₀ are optional extensions at the bottom.

A / IMPULSE RESPONSE & TIME SHIFT

One pulse becomes a sequence.

Across: time since the direct sound arrived. Up/down: signed response amplitude. The largest absolute amplitude is scaled to 1 so the shape is readable. A later spike means a delayed contribution; a small spike means a weaker contribution.

Why it matters

Convolution repeats this response for every speech sample. If h[n] extends in time, one input sample affects many output samples. That is how energy can continue into a pause.

Formula and modelling details

y[n] = Σ x[k]h[n−k]: multiply each input sample by a shifted response, then add. For a fixed position, the model is linear (doubling the input doubles the output) and time-invariant (sending the same sound later shifts the output by the same amount). The response includes an 80 Hz high-pass filter, which reduces very low-frequency components, so not every wiggle is a separate wall reflection. The graph shows the first 200 ms and preserves extrema within display bins.

B / ENERGY · SQUARE, THEN ADD

How much has arrived by this time?

Across: milliseconds after the first arrival. Up: percentage of the response’s total squared-sample energy collected so far. The curve only rises: squared values cannot be negative. The orange line is your chosen time boundary.

Why square samples? What are the units?

Adding signed samples can cancel a positive and negative swing. Squaring counts both: [1, −1] sums to 0, but its energy is 1² + (−1)² = 2. We use discrete signal energy E = Σ |h[n]|², in model units—not calibrated joules. Percentages divide by the total, removing scale. Moving the boundary changes our measurement, not the sound.

C / FOURIER TRANSFORM & DECIBELS

Which frequencies pass more strongly?

Across: frequency in hertz (cycles per second). Up: response magnitude relative to its strongest frequency. 0 dB marks that reference. −20 dB means one-tenth the amplitude, not “20% less”. A notch means stronger cancellation.

Why it matters

Speech contains many frequencies. Delayed copies reinforce some and cancel others, changing the balance of the sound. A room acts as a filter even when nobody adds an electronic equalizer.

Formula, FFT bins and normalization

The Fourier transform rewrites a signal as frequency components. Time convolution becomes Y(f) = X(f)H(f). A sufficiently zero-padded DFT gives the corresponding linear convolution. FFT is a fast way to compute a DFT. We plot 20 log₁₀(|H|/max|H|); each curve uses its own peak, so this view compares shape, not absolute loudness. Zero-padding creates a denser frequency grid without increasing physical resolving power.

Listening & alignment

Match RMS reduces overall signal-level differences for comparison. RMS means square the samples, average them, then take the square root; it is not a complete measure of perceived loudness.

Optional extension · what do C₅₀ and D₅₀ mean?

These are room-acoustics conventions, not topics we have verified in your syllabus. They apply the energy and logarithm ideas above using a fixed 50 ms boundary after direct arrival.

D₅₀ · early energy percentage

D₅₀ = 100 × early energy / total energy. It is a percentage of signal energy, not a percentage of words understood.

C₅₀ · early-to-late ratio in dB

dB

C₅₀ = 10 log₁₀(early energy / late energy). 0 dB means equal energy; +10 dB means ten times as much early as late energy.

These are two descriptions of the same split, not two independent proofs of quality. Measurements use the effective high-passed response and are not standardized octave-band classroom measurements.

Supplementary plot: energy in 5 ms bins, relative to the strongest bin. 0 dB is that bin; −30 dB is one-thousandth its energy. Green shading is the first 50 ms. The curve is clipped below −90 dB.

KEEP THESE DISTINCTIONS IN MIND

A small dictionary for this experiment

Amplitude vs energy
Amplitude is a signed sample value. Energy adds squared sample values. Doubling amplitude multiplies energy by four.
Delay vs spreading
Delay shifts when a signal begins. Spreading changes how long its effects continue. A signal can have one, the other, or both.
Hz vs samples/s
Hz describes cycles per second of a signal component. Samples/s describes how often we record a value. At 16,000 samples/s the Nyquist limit is 8,000 Hz. Frequencies above this limit must be removed before sampling, or they can appear as false lower frequencies (aliasing).
Decibels need a reference
0 dB does not mean silence. Here it means equality with the stated reference. Use 10 log₁₀ for energy ratios and 20 log₁₀ for amplitude ratios.
Simulation vs observation
These results are generated by a mathematical model. A measured room response and listener study would be needed to test real-world predictions.
A numerical test vs a hearing test
Code checks can catch a calculation error. They cannot establish that people understand speech better. We do not report an intelligibility score.

THE MODEL

A room is a filter

y[n] = x[n] ∗ h[n]

Each mirror image of the source contributes a delayed, attenuated copy of the signal. The 3-D model includes all six room surfaces.

Metrics and playback use an effective response with a fourth-order 80 Hz high-pass filter to remove low-frequency buildup from positive image pulses. This is a disclosed modelling choice, not a standardized octave-band measurement.

Pressure reflection coefficient: √(1 − α). Propagation: 343 m/s. Geometric spreading: 1/(4πd). Fractional delays use linear interpolation.

All surfaces share the same frequency-independent absorption. The floor-plan lines illustrate only a few paths.

LIMITS & NEXT STEPS

What we have not established

  • C₅₀ is an acoustic ratio, not a listener comprehension score.
  • No measured classroom validation, furniture, scattering, air absorption or human head effects.
  • Constant positive wall reflection and linear fractional delays simplify the frequency response.
  • Level-matched playback hides absolute level differences intentionally.
  • Next: measured impulse responses, frequency-dependent surfaces and blinded listening tests.
